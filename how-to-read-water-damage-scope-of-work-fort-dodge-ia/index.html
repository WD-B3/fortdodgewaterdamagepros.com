How to Read a Water Damage Scope of Work in Fort Dodge

A scope is a list of tasks with units. Learn the common line items and you can spot what is missing in a few minutes.

By the Fort Dodge Water Damage Pros team · Updated November 2026

A scope of work looks intimidating. It is full of abbreviations, quantities and codes. The good news is that it follows a pattern, and once you know the pattern you can read nearly any scope.

This guide explains common line items and what they mean. It is written for homeowners and property managers who want to understand what they are agreeing to.

The basic layout

Most scopes group tasks by room. Each line has a description, a quantity, a unit and a note. Units might be square feet, linear feet, each or hours. Totals come at the end. A good scope also includes notes explaining why a task is needed.

If a room has no entry, nothing is planned there. Check every wet room against the list.

Common line items explained

Line itemWhat it means
Water extractionRemoving standing water with pumps or extractors
Pad removalTaking out carpet cushion that cannot be dried
Flood cutCutting drywall above the water line to expose the cavity
Air moverHigh velocity fan used to dry surfaces
DehumidifierMachine that removes moisture from the air
AntimicrobialTreatment applied to cleaned surfaces
MonitoringDaily visits to check moisture readings
DemoRemoval of damaged materials

If you see a term you do not know, ask. The company should explain it plainly.

Quantities and units

Quantity is where scopes can hide problems. A flood cut of 24 linear feet, for example, should match the room. If a basement has 120 feet of wall and the scope says 12, ask how that was decided. Equipment days should match the plan too. Five days of drying for a flooded basement is common, but the number depends on the situation.

Do not assume the number is wrong. Ask for the reasoning. Good estimators can explain it quickly.

What is missing

  • Rooms you know are wet but are not listed.
  • Drying and monitoring without a stated end point.
  • Contents handling, such as pack-out, if you need it.
  • Rebuild tasks, if they are expected to be included.
  • Permits, if the work needs them.

Cross-check against your own photos. For details on the drying part, see structural drying. For ceilings and walls, see ceiling repair.

A sample walk through one room

Imagine a basement bedroom with a wet carpet. A sound scope might list water extraction by square foot, removal of carpet pad, tack strip and baseboard, application of antimicrobial treatment, air movers sized to the room, a dehumidifier for several days and daily monitoring visits.

Each line should tie to something you can see. If baseboard removal appears, you should be able to point at the baseboard. If the scope lists a flood cut, ask how high and how many feet.

If something seems excessive, ask for the reason. If something is missing, say so. Good estimators adjust when you give them facts.

Reading the totals and notes

The bottom of the scope shows totals, taxes where applicable and sometimes an overhead and profit line. Notes may state assumptions, such as access to the room or the type of water. Read those. They explain what the number depends on.

Check that the scope includes the dates of work or an expected duration. A drying job without a time frame leaves the end open.

Finally, check for any language that asks you to waive rights or accept responsibility for hidden conditions. Ask questions before signing.

Spotting padding and double counting

Two problems appear on some scopes. Padding is a line for work that is not needed, such as removing a floor that is dry. Double counting is the same task listed twice under different names, such as cleaning and disinfecting billed as separate items for the same surface.

You do not have to be an expert to catch these. Compare the scope to your photos, read each line against what you can see and ask about anything that does not match. A reasonable company can explain why each item is there.

If your insurer is paying, the adjuster will also review the scope. Share copies and ask them to point out items they question so you can discuss them with the contractor.

Questions to ask about the scope

  1. Does this scope match what the adjuster sees?
  2. What happens if more damage is found?
  3. Who confirms the structure is dry and how?
  4. When will I see the moisture logs?

You are allowed to ask these before signing. Reasonable companies welcome the conversation.

Most scope line items trace back to practices in the ANSI/IICRC S500 standard.

Fort Dodge note: documents to keep

Keep your signed scope, photos, moisture logs and receipts together in one folder. If a city office asks about repairs, a clear paper trail helps. In Fort Dodge, the Code Enforcement Division at 819 1st Ave S can say what documentation it needs for permitted work.

Questions from Fort Dodge homeowners

What is a flood cut?

It is a cut in drywall a little above the water line, made to expose and dry the wall cavity.

Why do scopes list equipment by the day?

Because drying takes time, and equipment is typically charged per day it runs.

Can the scope change after work starts?

Yes, if hidden damage is found. Ask for changes in writing.
